Social Trust, Institutional Confidence & Subjective Wellbeing

An end-to-end statistical study using the 2024 General Social Survey to examine relationships between interpersonal trust, confidence in institutions, and reported happiness among adults in the United States.

Research objective

The question behind the analysis

Does interpersonal trust remain associated with subjective wellbeing once demographic characteristics and survey design are considered?

This case study presents the study design, statistical approach and findings in a clear, independently reviewable format. Methodological qualifications are stated alongside the results.

What the evidence suggests
A descriptive difference was visible between the trust groups, but the adjusted ordinal model did not establish a statistically conclusive association.

This result illustrates the importance of separating interesting patterns from defensible inferential conclusions.

Methodology

How the research was carried out.

  1. Data-source verification and exclusion of the 677 supplementary AmeriSpeak cases from the primary sample
  2. Survey routing and valid-response audits, with 946 valid interpersonal-trust responses and 936 respondents answering both trust and happiness questions
  3. Weighted descriptive distributions using WTSSNRPS, stratification and primary sampling unit information
  4. Ordinal regression with age, recorded sex, educational attainment and marital status, supported by sensitivity checks and an unweighted benchmark
Interpretation and limitations. Observational, cross-sectional research. Survey routing reduced eligible sample sizes. Adjusted results are exploratory, with independent R/SPSS complex-survey replication still outstanding; no causal claims.
